# Conversion Copy

The canonical skill for any copy whose job is conversion. The spine is Harry Dry's 3-question filter. Around it sit 10 universal principles that apply to every channel, plus a channel-specific framework that gets loaded based on what's being written.

This skill exists because most conversion copy fails for the same reasons: it's abstract, unfalsifiable, signable by any competitor, written in brand-voice instead of customer-voice, and untethered from anything the reader actually said. The filter and principles below fix those failures.

## Non-negotiables

These apply every time this skill runs. Skipping any of them produces worse copy.

1. **Ground the copy in real customer language.** Conversion copy written from imagination almost always sounds like a brand wrote it. Before drafting, pull from real customer sources: user feedback and reviews, support messages, beta-tester and venue-owner conversations, store reviews of competing apps, Reddit/X/LinkedIn/Telegram comments where the reader describes the problem, and — importantly — Ali's own memory of things specific users or businesses have said. Ali often remembers the sharpest line verbatim; ask him. If sources aren't handed over and he doesn't surface anything from memory, ask before drafting (see "Asking for customer language" below). Don't paper over missing source material with invented phrases — the whole piece gets weaker when the voice is fake. And weight the language by who said it: a testimonial edit from someone outside the target ICP is a data point, not a veto — check the source before letting one client's phrasing overrule the copy.

2. **Verify proof claims against real data Ali provides.** Numbers in copy ("120 members," "40 venues," "events every week") get checked before they ship, not after. They come from data Ali hands over — a DB count, an analytics export, a store dashboard. Never invent a number, and never extrapolate one from a guess. If there is no data yet, write the line without the number or flag it as a placeholder. Quotes come only from real users who agreed to be quoted. Round claims down to what the data supports: "100+" that's true beats "120" that isn't, and a falsifiable claim that survives an audit is the whole point of the filter.

3. **Run every line through the central filter.** The three questions (visualize / falsify / unsignable) are non-negotiable. Every line passes all three, or it gets rewritten.

4. **Load the channel file before drafting.** The universal principles are always in play, but channel tactics matter most. Don't try to draft a LinkedIn post using landing-page logic, or a store listing using cold-email logic.

5. **Offer 2–3 versions when drafting, not one.** First-pass copy is almost never the best. Draft variants that point at different angles (e.g., one leads with the fear, one leads with the dream, one leads with the enemy), then state your top pick with reasoning. This is how Ali wants to iterate on copy — pre-selecting one version wastes Ali's time.

6. **Push back when the brief is thin.** Minimum viable brief = channel + offer + specific reader + customer-language source. If any are missing, ask before drafting. Don't paper over a weak brief with generic copy.

## Asking for customer language

Before drafting anything substantial, if Ali hasn't handed over customer-language source material, ask something like:

> *Before I write this, a few sources we should pull from: (1) any user feedback, reviews, or support messages you have handy, (2) Reddit/X/LinkedIn/Telegram comments where your reader is describing this exact problem, (3) specific things you remember users or venue owners saying in person or in DMs — even one or two verbatim lines is gold. Want to grab anything from those, or walk me through what you've heard from users in your own words?*

Good sources for Ali's world:
- Beta-tester and member feedback on the app, and what venue owners said when pitched
- App Store / Google Play reviews of competing apps (what people praise, what they complain about)
- Telegram groups and channels where the reader hangs out, including Persian-language ones
- Reddit, X, and LinkedIn threads where the reader describes the problem in their own words
- For freelance or partner outreach: what past clients said about the work, and the prospect's own job posts or public statements
- **Ali's own memory.** If Ali can recall a line verbatim or paraphrased from a real conversation, that's as good as a transcript — ask.

Even on a first draft for a brand-new offer, spend the extra minute pulling from one of these. Invented customer-voice is the single most common reason conversion copy falls flat; it's cheaper to dig for 30 seconds than to rewrite the whole piece after the fact.

If Ali genuinely can't source any customer language (e.g., the offer is pre-launch and no one has seen it yet), say so explicitly in the draft and flag the lines that are guessed so he knows which ones to pressure-test later.

## The central filter — Harry Dry's 3 questions

Every line of conversion copy — headline, subhead, bullet, CTA, opening line — must pass all three:

1. **Can I visualize it?** Concrete beats abstract. If the reader can't see it in their head, it won't stick. *"Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio"* passes. *"Worn by diverse customers"* fails.

2. **Can I falsify it?** True-or-false beats subjective. Specific numbers, named people, provable claims. *"Cut our monthly reporting from 6 hours to 20 minutes"* passes. *"Saves you tons of time"* fails. You can't bullshit specifics.

3. **Can nobody else say it?** Differentiation. If a competitor could sign the same line, it's not copy — it's noise. *"Invite-only nights at venues you can't book on your own"* is hard for a generic events app to sign. *"Discover amazing events near you"* could come from anyone.

Three yes → the line is working. Any no → rewrite.

When **diagnosing** existing copy, score each key line against each question with ✓/✗ and a one-line reason. When **drafting** new copy, hold the filter in your head as you write and only ship lines that pass. Don't present copy that fails the filter without saying so.

## Universal principles

Ten principles that apply regardless of channel. Full treatment and examples in `references/universal-principles.md`. Summary:

1. **Write for one reader.** Not 1,000. One. "You" over "users." Specific ICP over broad demographic.
2. **Use the customer's voice.** The copy reads like the customer wrote it. Mine their words; don't invent yours.
3. **Sell outcomes, not features.** *"Ace the SAT"* beats *"next-gen SAT prep."* *"Brighter smile"* beats *"better toothbrush."*
4. **Specific beats vague.** Kill adjectives and adverbs. Replace "fast" with "ships in 48 hours." You can't bullshit specifics.
5. **Cut ruthlessly.** Kaplan's Law: any word not working for you is working against you. A great sentence is a good sentence made shorter.
6. **Write how you talk.** Contractions, conjunctions to start sentences, short sentences. Read it aloud; if it sounds written, rewrite.
7. **Don't persuade — let them be persuaded.** Advice > pitch. Show, don't tell. Point at artifacts instead of making claims.
8. **Create momentum.** First line earns the second. Every line pulls the reader to the next ("slippery slide").
9. **Memorable beats likeable.** Clever-and-forgettable loses to plain-and-sticky. Jobs shrank 9 pages of Lisa spec to "Think different."

Lean on these over the ~50-tip tactical reference. The universals do most of the work. Pull `references/universal-principles.md` when you need the reasoning or examples; pull `references/tactical-tips.md` only for specific checks (e.g., "is this CTA conversational enough?").

## Persian audiences

When the reader is Persian, write natively in Persian. Don't draft in English and translate: English idioms, puns, and sentence rhythm don't survive the trip, and a translated headline reads like one. Run the same 3-question filter on the Persian line itself. For localising copy between fa and en (register, RTL, numerals, half-spaces), defer to the `bilingual-copy` skill.

## The two modes

### Review mode — Ali pastes existing copy

Workflow:
1. Confirm the channel and reader if unclear.
2. Run the 3-question filter on each key line (headline, subhead, CTA, key body lines). Score ✓/✗ with a one-line reason.
3. Check against the universal principles — flag the top 2–3 violations, not all 9.
4. Return the diagnosis first (what's working, what's not, why), then 2–3 rewrites of the weakest lines with reasoning.

Don't silently rewrite. The diagnosis is valuable — it teaches Ali the filter and lets Ali audit your rewrites.

### Draft mode — Ali briefs an offer / channel

Workflow:
1. Lock the brief (channel, offer, reader, customer-language source). Ask if anything is missing.
2. Load the channel file.
3. Draft 2–3 versions, each pointing at a different angle. Make the angle explicit ("this one leads with the pain point," "this one leads with the outcome," "this one leads with the objection").
4. State your top pick with reasoning.
5. Iterate section by section for long-form work (landing pages, venue pitches). Don't one-shot a full landing page — lock the headline first, then the subhead, etc.

### Propagate winners across surfaces

When a line gets locked on one funnel surface, ask which sibling surfaces carry the same promise (homepage hero, store listing, onboarding screen, venue pitch email) and flag them for the same update. A reader who hits the promise twice in the same words experiences one voice; a funnel where each surface phrases the offer differently reads as three different pitches. This also keeps A/B attribution clean — one promise change at a time, everywhere it appears, beats surfaces drifting independently.

Non-negotiable for Ali's voice:

- **Smart, no-fluff, high-status.** Peer-to-peer with busy skeptics. No hype. No hand-holding.
- **Kill "landing page words."** Unleash, unlock, supercharge, empower, enhance, exceed, elevate, transform, revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, next-gen (unless ironic), seamless, robust, leverage (as a verb). Real people don't talk this way. If a word feels like it belongs on a SaaS homepage, it doesn't belong in Ali's copy.
- **Light on em-dashes.** They read as AI-written. Use commas, periods, or parentheses. One or two per page is fine — more than that and you're overusing.
- **Visceral verbs over soft verbs.** "Delete 20 hours" beats "save 20 hours." "Reclaim your week" beats "get your week back." "Kill the bottleneck" beats "reduce the bottleneck."
- **Concrete over abstract.** "Rooftop dinner, 20 seats, Thursday" beats "exclusive experiences." Always go one level more specific than feels necessary.
- **No default reader.** Ali sells to different people: the app's members, venue and business owners, app-store browsers, freelance clients. Ask which one if the brief doesn't say. Don't write for "everyone."

## Handoffs

This skill complements others. Clear lanes:

- **General email drafts without a conversion goal** → `email-helper`.
- **Docs, READMEs, posts that aren't selling anything** → `writing-style`.
- **Translating or localising existing copy between Persian and English** → `bilingual-copy`.
- **Live price or terms pushback from a venue or client** → `tactical-negotiation`.
- **Whether the offer itself is right (pricing, who to target)** → `business-strategy`.

If Ali is clearly in the wrong skill, say so and suggest the right one. Don't try to do a neighboring skill's job.

## One last thing

Conversion copy is selling. Don't romanticize it. The goal isn't to be clever or cute. The goal is to inspire action. If your draft reads beautifully but doesn't move the reader toward the next step, it's failing its only job.
