# Channel: App Store / Google Play / Telegram Bot Listings

Use this framework when writing or reviewing a store listing: an App Store page, a Google Play page, or a Telegram bot's name, description, and about text. The job is one action: install, or press Start.

This is **not** the framework for the product's website (→ `channel-landing-pages.md`). A listing is read in seconds, inside a store full of competitors, and most of it is never expanded.

## Before you draft

Lock these:

- **Store and its limits.** Check the current character limits before drafting, since they change. Roughly: App Store title and subtitle are short (around 30 characters each), Google Play has a short description (around 80) and a long one, and a Telegram bot has a short description shown before Start and a longer about text.
- **What people search for.** The words a reader would type to find this. Those words go in the title or subtitle, not buried in the long description.
- **The one reason to install.** Not the feature list. The one thing that makes someone stop scrolling.
- **Language.** One listing per language. A Persian listing is written in Persian, not translated (see `bilingual-copy`).

## Core principles (listing-specific)

### 1. Title = name + what it is

A name alone tells a browser nothing. Pair it with the plainest description of the thing, using a word people search for.

- **Weak:** "Nightly"
- **Strong:** "Nightly: Members-Only Events"

### 2. Subtitle / short description = the outcome

This is the store's equivalent of a landing-page subhead. Sell the outcome, run it through the 3-question filter.

- **Weak:** "The best way to discover experiences"
- **Strong:** "Invite-only nights at venues near you"

### 3. Only the first 3 lines get read

Stores fold the long description after a few lines. Those lines carry the whole pitch: who it's for, what they get, why this one. Everything below the fold is for the few who expand it and for search.

- Line 1: the outcome, in the reader's words.
- Line 2: the proof or the gate (what makes it unsignable).
- Line 3: what happens after install, or the CTA.

### 4. Screenshots are copy

Most people decide on the screenshots, not the text. Each caption is one short line that says what the screen gives them, not what the screen is.

- **Weak:** "Event details screen"
- **Strong:** "See who's going before you say yes"

Order the screenshots like a story: the outcome first, then how it works, then the proof.

### 5. Telegram bots: the Start message is the landing page

The short description sells the press on Start. The first message after Start has to deliver something at once, or ask one question. A wall of commands is a bounce.

- **Weak first message:** "Welcome! Here are my commands: /help /search /settings /about ..."
- **Strong first message:** "Send me a link and I'll send back the file. Try one now."

## Which universal principles matter most here

- **#5 Cut ruthlessly** — the limits do half the cutting for you. Use what's left on outcome, not adjectives.
- **#4 Specific beats vague** — "near you, tonight" beats "amazing experiences."
- **#3 Sell outcomes, not features** — the feature list goes below the fold.

## Common failure modes

1. **A name with no description.** Nobody searches for a brand they haven't heard of.
2. **Feature lists above the fold.** "Profiles, events, chat, notifications" says nothing about why.
3. **Screenshot captions that label the screen.** Say what the screen gives the reader.
4. **One listing machine-translated into another language.** It reads like one, and reviews say so.
5. **Claims the reviews will contradict.** "Thousands of members" on a new app costs more trust than it buys. Use only numbers Ali can back.
