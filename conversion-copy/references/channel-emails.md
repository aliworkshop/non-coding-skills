# Channel: Sales Emails (warm)

Use this framework when writing a 1:1 sales email to someone who already knows Ali — a venue or business owner after a demo, a freelance or consulting client after a first call, a past client worth re-engaging, a referral whose context is clear, or someone who went quiet.

This is **not** the framework for cold outreach to strangers (→ `channel-outreach.md`) or general email drafts without a conversion intent (→ `email-helper`).

## Before you draft

Lock these:

- **Who is this person and what's the context?** Past client? Went-quiet prospect? Post-demo venue owner?
- **What's the single next step?** Reply with a date? Set up their venue page? Pay the invoice? Confirm the proposal?
- **Is there recent history you can reference?** A specific thing said on the call, a recent LinkedIn post of theirs, a project they mentioned. Use it.
- **Customer-language source.** If it's a past client, their own quotes are the best material.

## Core principles (warm-email-specific)

### 1. Short wins

Dave Gerhardt's 1-line sales email works because most sales emails suffer from bloat. When you have the warm context, you don't need to re-establish anything.

- If the email could be three lines, don't write five.
- The goal is to get a reply, not to pre-close the deal. A reply gives you something to work with.
- Long setup paragraphs signal uncertainty. Short direct emails signal confidence.

### 2. Ask a question

Questions create reply-pressure in a way statements don't. The whole email can be one question plus signature.

- Example (Dave Gerhardt style): *"Hey [name] — quick one. We're opening 5 venue spots for next month's members-only nights. Want one?"*
- The question should make "no" easy and "yes" specific.

### 3. Reference the specific context

The warm email's superpower is that you can reference things cold emails can't:

- "You mentioned on our call that X was eating your week..."
- "Following up on that question about Y..."
- "Saw your post about Z — thought of you because..."

If you have the context, use it. If you don't, ask why you're sending a "warm" email without it.

### 4. Advice beats pitch

Same as cold outreach, even with warm leads. Ask for input, offer a next step that's useful to *them* regardless of whether they buy.

- "I've been thinking about the situation you described — here's what I'd try first. Want me to walk through it in 15 minutes?"
- That framing makes the next step valuable *to the reader* whether or not they end up buying.

### 5. Personalize the opening line (it becomes preview text)

Gmail/Apple Mail show the first line as preview. A generic "Hi [name], I wanted to reach out about..." burns the preview. A specific opener gets opens.

- **Burns preview:** "Hi Ali, I wanted to reach out to follow up on our previous conversation..."
- **Uses preview well:** "Ali — quick one about the slow Tuesdays you mentioned..."

### 6. Match the commitment level to the relationship

Don't ask for a 60-minute meeting if a 1-sentence reply will do. The CTA should feel proportional to where the relationship is.

- **Post-demo warm lead:** "Want me to set up your page?" (they already know the offer)
- **Past client:** "Worth a 15-minute catch-up?" (low friction, no new context needed)
- **Ghosted prospect:** "Still on your radar, or should I stop bugging you?" (gives them permission to say no, which often surfaces a yes)

## Structure (default — ultra-lean)

Most warm sales emails should be 3–5 short paragraphs max. Structure:

1. **Opener that uses recent context** (1 line).
2. **The specific thing you're following up on / offering** (1–2 lines).
3. **The ask — a question they can answer in a reply** (1 line).
4. **Sign-off** (— Ali).

If your email is longer than this, ask what's doing the extra work.

## Which universal principles matter most here

- **#5 Cut ruthlessly** — warm emails live or die on brevity.
- **#6 Write how you talk** — conversational tone, contractions, short sentences.
- **#7 Don't persuade** — advice-framed outreach consistently outperforms pitch-framed.
- **#2 Use the customer's voice** — quote their words back to them when relevant.

## Voice cues

- First-name basis is fine (assumed from warm context).
- Don't formally sign — "— Ali" beats "Best regards, Ali Torabi." In Persian, a short "ممنون، علی" beats a formal letter closing.
- No subject-line gimmicks. "Quick follow-up" or "Your venue page" or "[Name] — one question" work.
- Read it aloud. If it sounds like you're dictating a formal letter, rewrite.

## Worked example — follow-up to a venue owner

**Context:** Ali showed the app's business side to Sara, who runs a small rooftop restaurant. She liked it and said she'd "think about it."

**Weak draft:**
> *Hi Sara,*
>
> *I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out to follow up on our conversation from earlier this week. As I mentioned, our platform is designed to help venues like yours unlock new audiences and transform your business.*
>
> *I would love to answer any questions you may have. Please let me know if you have availability for a follow-up conversation.*
>
> *Best regards,*
> *Ali*

**Filter check:** First line burns preview. Landing page words everywhere. No specific reference to the meeting. Generic CTA. The whole message is neutral — could have been sent to anyone.

**Strong draft:**

> Sara — you said Tuesdays are the night you can't fill. I sketched what a members-only Tuesday would look like on the app for your rooftop.
>
> Want me to send 3 screenshots of how it would appear to members? No call needed.
>
> If it lands, reply "in" and I'll set up your venue page.
>
> — Ali

**Why this works:**
- Uses preview text ("Sara — you said Tuesdays...").
- References specific, verifiable meeting context.
- "I sketched" signals actual thought, not a template.
- Offers a next step that's useful even if she doesn't sign up (she can show the screenshots to a partner).
- Two-step CTA (look at screenshots, then reply "in"). Lower friction than "let's schedule a call."
- Short. Direct. Doesn't rehash the pitch.

## Pricing, discount & objection replies

When a serious buyer asks the price, asks for a discount, or raises an objection, the reply's job is to hold value and advance the deal — not to answer literally and stop. The repeatable move:

1. **State the price plainly, hold the line.** Give the number without hedging. If they asked for a discount, decline it directly ("I won't be able to do a discount"), then immediately pivot to levers that lower *cost* without cutting *price* — a smaller first milestone, fewer features, a shorter trial. You protect the rate while still giving them a path to yes.
2. **Recommend the highest-leverage starting point.** Don't hand them a flat menu. Point at where the ROI is most obvious ("I'd start with the payments integration, that's where you lose money today").
3. **Anchor with a concrete value proof.** Tie price to a tangible outcome in customer language. Use a number from real past work only, never a guess. Run it through the 3-question filter — visualizable, falsifiable, unsignable.
4. **Offer a scope expansion when it fits.** A pricing question is often the moment to widen the deal, not just size it ("I could also take on the admin panel once the API is done").
5. **Close soft and open.** "What do you think?" beats "Just say the word" while they're still deciding — it invites the next message rather than presuming the close.

**Worked example (freelance discount ask):**

> While I won't be able to lower the rate, here are a couple of ways we could get the cost down.
>
> 1. Smaller first milestone: the API and the database only, and your team builds the admin panel.
> 2. Fixed scope: we freeze the feature list now, so nothing gets added mid-way and billed.
>
> I'd start with option 1. The API is where a mistake costs you money later, and it's the part I can hand over with tests and docs.

For live back-and-forth on price, use the `tactical-negotiation` skill.
>
> What do you think?

Why it works: holds the rate, reframes "discount" as "cost-down options," steers to the highest-ROI starting point, anchors the price against a falsifiable outcome, and ends on an open question that keeps the thread moving.

## Common failure modes

1. **"I hope this email finds you well" opener.** Burns preview text and signals a template.
2. **Re-pitching what was already pitched on the call.** If you sold it on the call, don't re-sell — address objections or lower the friction to the next step.
3. **"Let me know if you have any questions."** This is a dead close. Replace with a specific question.
4. **Long emails to post-call warm leads.** They already know the offer. Say the specific new thing, then stop.
5. **Formal sign-offs.** "Best regards, Ali Torabi" reads as a form letter. "— Ali" reads as a person.
6. **CTA asking for another call when a reply would do.** Don't escalate commitment unless you have to.
